You will not train these models from scratch, and you will not choose the physics in them. Your work is downstream of both. It comes in two kinds: make the models fast on machines you operate, and make them easy to retrain for scientists who bring their own data. Everything in this chapter serves one of those two jobs. First, the contract they both depend on.

## Part 0: The contract

Strip away the chemistry and a machine-learning interatomic potential is a pure function.

The input is a configuration of atoms. Each atom has an atomic number — an integer between 1 and about 100 that says which element it is — and a position, three floating-point coordinates in angstroms. An angstrom is a tenth of a nanometer, about the size of an atom. For crystals and liquids there is also a three-by-three matrix describing the box the atoms live in, because the box repeats forever in all directions; this is the periodic cell. A few newer models also accept a total charge and a spin state for the whole system.

The output is one scalar: the potential energy of the whole configuration, in electron-volts. Alongside it, one three-vector per atom: the force on that atom, in electron-volts per angstrom. Sometimes also a three-by-three stress tensor — the force analog for the box itself, used when the simulation is allowed to change the box's shape or volume.

The forces are not a second output head. The force on an atom is how much the energy drops when that atom moves — the negative gradient of the energy with respect to that atom's position. Most models compute it the way you would compute any gradient: run the forward pass to get the energy, then run autograd back to the position inputs. So "inference," for these models, means a forward pass *and* a backward pass. Plan capacity accordingly.

The caller is almost never a human. It is a loop. In molecular dynamics, the loop asks for forces, nudges every atom along its force for about one femtosecond of simulated time, and asks again — millions of times per trajectory. In a relaxation, the loop moves atoms downhill in energy and stops when the largest force falls below a threshold, typically after tens to hundreds of calls; and a relaxation campaign often runs thousands of independent structures at once. The two callers stress the model differently, and the difference drives most of Part 1.

The universal socket is the ASE `Calculator`: a Python object with `get_potential_energy` and `get_forces`, attached to an `Atoms` object. Every model family ships one, which is why Rootstock can serve them all behind one interface. For large parallel runs the socket is a LAMMPS pair style instead — LAMMPS owns the atoms and the parallelism, and calls the model as a plugin.

> **[FIG]** The contract as a box diagram: atomic numbers, positions, cell, (charge, spin) flowing in; energy, forces, stress flowing out; a loop arrow from forces back to positions labeled "the caller is a simulation loop, not a person."

**So if you're building the library, this implies:** the unit you serve is a calculator, and the service-level target is per-call latency inside a tight loop, not throughput on independent requests. Anything paid per call — process startup, checkpoint load, graph capture — must be hoisted out of the loop and paid once. And "inference hardware" must be sized for forward plus backward, not forward alone.

## Part 1: The job of making it fast

Start with where one molecular-dynamics step spends its time.

**Neighbor list.** The model only lets atoms within a cutoff radius — 5 to 6 angstroms in practice — interact directly. So before anything else, someone must find, for each atom, its neighbors within the cutoff: typically 20 to 80 of them. Comparing every atom against every other grows with the square of the atom count; binning atoms into a grid of cells and checking only adjacent cells grows linearly. And the list is not rebuilt every step: add a margin to the cutoff, called the skin, and the list stays valid until some atom has moved half the skin distance. Amortized over steps, the neighbor list is cheap — unless you rebuild it every step by accident, which is a real and common way to lose half your budget.

**Graph assembly.** The neighbor list becomes a graph: atoms are nodes, neighbor pairs are edges. Concretely, two integer tensors of source and destination indices, plus a shift vector per edge recording which periodic image of the box the neighbor sits in. This is bookkeeping, but it runs every step and it touches the CPU-to-GPU boundary.

**Forward pass.** One shape, across every family. Each atom's atomic number indexes an embedding table — a vocabulary of about 100, which after transformer vocabularies feels like a rounding error. Each edge is described by its length, expanded in a radial basis (a soft one-hot over distance), and its direction, expanded in spherical harmonics (a basis over orientations; the reason these models respect rotation). Then a small number of interaction blocks — two to six, not dozens — in which each atom gathers messages from its neighbors: tensor products combine the edge's directional features with the neighbor's features, small matrix multiplies mix channels, and a scatter-add sums the messages into the atom's new state. Finally a small readout network maps each atom's state to a per-atom energy, and a sum over atoms gives the one scalar. SchNet does this with distances only; NequIP carries directional features through the blocks; MACE builds higher-body-order products so it needs only about two blocks; EquiformerV2 and eSEN put attention inside each neighborhood. The skeleton is the same.

**Backward pass.** Autograd from the energy back to the positions, for forces. Comparable in cost to the forward.

**Integration.** Updating positions and velocities is a handful of vector operations. Ignore it.

Now the character of the workload, because it is not the one you know from transformers. A transformer forward is a few enormous matrix multiplies; the GPU is busy. An MLIP forward is scatters, gathers, and many *small* matrix multiplies, because the tensors are sized by atoms and edges, not by a 4,096-wide hidden dimension. Bespoke models run around a hundred thousand to a million parameters; MACE-MP-0 medium is 4.7 million; most foundation checkpoints stay under a hundred million active, though UMA-medium reaches 1.4 billion total with about 50 million active per structure. At a few hundred atoms, the GPU spends much of each step waiting for the CPU to launch the next tiny kernel. That fact selects the levers.

**Lever one: batch independent structures.** Graphs compose block-diagonally: concatenate the atoms of many structures into one tensor, offset each structure's edge indices, keep a vector recording which structure each atom belongs to, and one forward pass evaluates them all. Kernel launches amortize; the small matrix multiplies become bigger ones. A relaxation farm — ten thousand candidate crystals, each stepping downhill — is the perfect customer. A single long MD trajectory is the worst one: step one million and one depends on step one million, so it cannot be batched with itself. This asymmetry is the first thing to establish about any workload you are asked to speed up.

**Lever two: stop recompiling.** `torch.compile` and CUDA graphs assume fixed tensor shapes. But the edge count changes every step, because atoms drift in and out of each other's cutoff spheres. Naively, every step is a new shape and a recompile, and the "optimized" path runs slower than eager mode. The fix is padding to shape buckets: round the atom and edge counts up to the next of a handful of preset sizes, mask out the padding, and now there are five compilations for the whole run instead of one per step. The padding wastes a few percent of arithmetic. The bucket cache earns it back many times over.

**Lever three: fuse the hot kernel.** The tensor product at the heart of the equivariant families — the Clebsch–Gordan product, which combines directional edge features with node features without breaking rotational symmetry — decomposes into a swarm of tiny multiplies, one per allowed combination of angular channels. In plain PyTorch that swarm is a swarm of kernel launches. Fused implementations — NVIDIA's cuEquivariance is the current one to know — do it in a few kernels. On NequIP- and MACE-shaped models this is often the single largest win at medium-to-large atom counts; at small counts, killing kernel-launch overhead (CUDA graphs) can matter more.

**Lever four: choose precision by conservation, not by static error.** The default is float32. TF32 makes matrix multiplies faster but adds noise to forces, and the metric that catches the damage is not force error on a held-out set — it is whether the total energy of a closed simulation drifts over a million steps. Forces that are slightly inconsistent with the energy act like a tiny hidden heater or refrigerator: invisible in any single step, ruinous over a trajectory. Tight relaxations and vibrational-property runs often step up to float64 for the same reason. Precision is a per-workload knob with a physics-facing acceptance test, not a global config flag.

**Lever five: predict forces directly.** Some models add an output head that emits forces, skipping the backward pass entirely — roughly halving the step. Orb-v3 ships direct and conservative variants side by side; UMA and eSEN train a direct head internally but release conservative checkpoints only — check what a family actually ships before exposing the option. The price: predicted forces are no longer exactly the gradient of the predicted energy, so nothing guarantees energy conservation, and long MD trajectories drift. For relaxations and high-throughput screening, where each structure gets a short burst of calls and nobody integrates for a nanosecond, direct forces are close to a free win. For long dynamics they are a liability. This is a product decision to expose per workload, not a default to bake in.

**Lever six: strict locality for scale-out.** Message passing widens what an atom can see: after each interaction block, information has traveled one more cutoff radius. Four blocks at 6 angstroms means every atom's energy depends on atoms 24 angstroms away. On one GPU that costs nothing extra. Across nodes it is the whole bill: domain decomposition — LAMMPS's parallelism — gives each rank a spatial region plus a halo of ghost atoms copied from neighboring ranks, and the halo must be as thick as the receptive field. Thick halos mean each rank spends its time communicating copies instead of computing. Allegro is built strictly local — every atom's energy contribution depends only on its own neighborhood, one cutoff, regardless of depth — so its halos stay thin and it scales across many nodes. When someone asks for a billion-atom run, the model choice *is* the systems choice.

**So if you're building the library, this implies:** the serving process must be persistent and warm — checkpoint loaded, buckets compiled, graphs captured — because every lever above is an amortization play. Batching should be a first-class API for relaxation and screening customers, and explicitly refused for single-trajectory MD rather than silently useless. Precision and force mode belong in the request schema, each with a documented default and the acceptance test that justifies it.

## Part 2: The job of making fine-tuning easy

A user shows up with a foundation checkpoint and a folder of their own quantum-mechanics calculations. Your tooling stands between them and a working model. Know what is in the checkpoint first.

**Anatomy of a checkpoint.** An element embedding table, about 100 rows. Radial networks, small multilayer perceptrons that shape how interaction strength varies with distance. The tensor-product weights inside each interaction block — the bulk of the parameters. The readout networks. And one unglamorous vector that breaks more transfers than everything else combined: the per-element reference energies.

Here is why they exist. The raw energies that density functional theory produces are enormous, and almost all of that magnitude is just the cost of the electrons in each isolated atom — the same for every configuration containing those atoms. The interesting part, the part that differs between a good crystal and a bad one, is a small remainder. So every model subtracts a fixed baseline energy per element and learns only the remainder. The trap: those baselines depend on the DFT recipe — the functional, the pseudopotentials, the code's settings. If the user's labels were computed with a different recipe than the pretraining data, their raw energies sit on a different baseline, offset by a constant per element. Forces are unaffected — a constant has zero gradient — so the fine-tune *looks* healthy while every energy comparison across compositions is silently wrong. The fix is mechanical: refit the per-element references to the user's data, which is a linear least-squares solve, before or during training. Tooling should do this without being asked.

**The loss.** A weighted sum of energy error, force error, and stress error, with the force weight high. Two reasons: a snapshot with N atoms carries one energy label but three-N force labels, so forces are where the information is; and simulation quality follows force quality. The consequence for your GPU budget is easy to miss. The predicted force is already a derivative — energy with respect to positions. Training on force labels means differentiating that derivative with respect to the weights: second-order autograd. Rule of thumb, two to three times the memory and time of energy-only training. A checkpoint you can *serve* on a modest card may need the largest card you have to *fine-tune*.

That second-order backward also collides with the inference fast path. The fused equivariant kernels and `torch.compile`/CUDA-graph capture that make serving fast do not currently support double-backward (NVIDIA's cuEquivariance issue #268), so the exact kernels you reach for on the serving side often have to be dropped or run eager for force-label fine-tuning. If you own both stacks, that is two code paths through the same hot kernel, not one — budget for it.

**The data.** A few hundred to a few thousand DFT snapshots is the norm, not a compromise — each label costs CPU-hours to compute, and the pretraining already bought generality (UMA's five FAIR datasets total roughly 500 million structures; the user is buying the last mile). What matters more than volume is consistency: every snapshot computed with the same functional and settings as every other, and reconciled with the base model's recipe by the reference refit above. One snapshot from an old project with different settings, mixed in silently, is a mislabeled example the loss cannot detect.

**Forgetting, and the replay fix.** Fine-tune a general-purpose potential on five hundred snapshots of one zeolite and its general chemistry degrades — the same catastrophic forgetting you know from LLM fine-tuning, with the same fix: replay. Mix a slice of the pretraining distribution into the fine-tuning batches so the old capabilities keep getting gradient. Some families ship this as tooling — MACE's fine-tuning path replays a slice of its MPtrj pretraining set by default.

**What "it worked" means.** Energy and force error on held-out snapshots is necessary and insufficient. A model can post good static numbers and still melt a solid at room temperature, because errors that are small pointwise can be systematically wrong in the rare configurations a trajectory visits. The real acceptance test is dynamical: run MD from the fine-tuned model, confirm it is stable — no explosion, no energy drift — and confirm it reproduces a number the user already trusts: a lattice constant, a density, a radial distribution function. Budget hours of GPU time for the acceptance run and treat it as part of the fine-tune, not an optional epilogue.

**Where the LLM playbook ports, and where it doesn't.** Full fine-tuning at a small learning rate is the field's norm — these models are small enough that parameter memory is not the constraint; second-order autograd is. That is why LoRA, which exists to dodge parameter memory, is rare here so far; if applied, it would target the linear and tensor-product weights. The field's version of multi-task heads is UMA's Mixture of Linear Experts: a router conditioned on the dataset or task, and on charge and spin, blends the weights of linear experts, so one backbone serves several DFT recipes without one checkpoint per recipe. If you are building multi-tenant fine-tuning infrastructure, that design is the direction of travel.

**So if you're building the tooling, this implies:** automate the parts users reliably get wrong — validate label consistency across the uploaded snapshots, refit per-element references by default, mix in replay data by default. Size the fine-tuning tier at two to three times serving memory. And make the final artifact of every fine-tuning run an MD stability report, not a loss curve.

## Part 3: A gotcha ledger

Six entries. Each has ended someone's week.

**Units.** Everything in this world is electron-volts and angstroms; forces are electron-volts per angstrom. Stress arrives in electron-volts per cubic angstrom but gets reported in gigapascals; the conversion factor is about 160. The biomolecular world runs on kilocalories per mole — one electron-volt is about 23 of them — and numbers imported from that world without conversion are wrong by that factor. Check units at every boundary you own.

**Periodic boundaries and the minimum image.** In a periodic box, the distance between two atoms is the distance to the *nearest copy* of the neighbor, possibly in an adjacent image of the box; each edge carries a shift vector saying which image. Bugs here hide in large boxes and surface in small ones — when the cutoff exceeds half the box length, an atom can neighbor several images of the same atom, and code that assumed one edge per pair quietly drops interactions.

**Who owns the neighbor list.** Behind an ASE calculator, the model builds its own list. Under LAMMPS, the host builds one and hands it over. Get the ownership wrong and you either build the list twice — pure waste — or run with the host's cutoff disagreeing with the model's, which corrupts forces at the cutoff boundary without crashing anything.

**Checkpoint and version drift.** A family name is not a model. Every family ships successive checkpoints, and the code that loads them changes defaults between releases; a checkpoint evaluated under a different code version than it was trained against can differ subtly or refuse to load. Pin the triple — family, code version, checkpoint — and treat it as one immutable artifact. This is precisely what a Rootstock environment is.

**Batching index bugs.** When concatenating graphs, every structure's edge indices must be offset by the running atom count. Get an offset wrong and energies still look plausible — they are sums, and sums are forgiving — while forces leak between structures that should not know about each other. The parity test is non-negotiable: a batch of structures must reproduce the one-at-a-time results to tight tolerance, in continuous integration, forever.

**Silent extrapolation.** The model outputs confident numbers for any input, including configurations nothing in training resembled. The signature failure: a trajectory runs beautifully for a million steps, then a rare thermal fluctuation reaches a configuration outside the training distribution, the forces there are garbage, and the run explodes — or, worse, doesn't explode and delivers wrong physics with a straight face. There is no exception to catch. Run watchdogs: a maximum-force threshold, a temperature monitor, an energy-drift check, and where a family offers an uncertainty signal, surface it.

**So if you're building the infrastructure, this implies:** the ledger is your test plan. Unit-checked boundaries, pinned environment triples, batched-versus-single parity in CI, and watchdogs attached to every managed run are not hardening for later — they are the product, because every entry above fails silently, and silent failure in a simulation is indistinguishable from science.
